A junction point lies on both lines, so its coordinates must satisfy both equations at once. That is precisely a pair of simultaneous equations. There should be a routine way to find the shared point — and a way to tell in advance whether one even exists.

3The mathematics

One point that satisfies both

A point where two lines meet is a point that lies on both lines. In equation language that means its (x, y) makes both equations true at the same time.

The intersection is a simultaneous solution

A point is on both lines if and only if it satisfies both equations. So finding where two lines meet is exactly solving two equations simultaneously.

Method 1 — substitution

Make one variable the subject of one equation, then substitute it into the other.

from 2x − y = 1:   y = 2x − 1   ⇒   put into x + y = 4

Method 2 — elimination

Multiply one or both equations so a coefficient matches, then add or subtract to wipe out a variable.

2x − y = 1   +   (x + y = 4)   ⇒   3x = 5

The three outcomes

OutcomeSlopesWhat the algebra does
One solutiondifferentThe lines meet once; you get a real (x, y).
No solutionequal, different interceptThe lines are parallel; the algebra collapses to a false statement like 0 = 7.
Infinitely manyequal, same lineThe equations describe one line; the algebra collapses to a true statement like 0 = 0.

Recognising it from the coefficients

For two lines written as a₁x + b₁y + c₁ = 0 and a₂x + b₂y + c₂ = 0, form

D = a₁b₂ − a₂b₁
  • D ≠ 0 ⇒ the lines have different slopes ⇒ one solution (they meet once).
  • D = 0 and a₁c₂ − a₂c₁ ≠ 0 ⇒ equal slopes, different line ⇒ parallel, no solution.
  • D = 0 and the constants are also proportional ⇒ the same line, infinitely many shared points.

In the y = mx + c language

If the lines are y = m₁x + c₁ and y = m₂x + c₂:

  • One solution iff m₁ ≠ m₂.
  • Parallel iff m₁ = m₂ and c₁ ≠ c₂.
  • Identical iff m₁ = m₂ and c₁ = c₂.
  • Perpendicular iff m₁m₂ = −1.
Why two lines can meet at most once

Two distinct straight lines cannot bend to meet a second time. So there are only three possibilities: never (parallel), exactly once, or always (the same line). That is why the algebra can only ever produce one point, a contradiction, or an identity.

5Worked examples

Doing it properly, on paper

Try each one yourself first — write it down. Only then open the solution.

Example 1 — y = 2x − 1 and y = −x + 5

Both already give y, so set them equal (substitution):

2x − 1 = −x + 5  ⇒  3x = 6  ⇒  x = 2

Substitute back: y = 2(2) − 1 = 3. The lines meet at (2, 3).

Example 2 — x + 2y = 8 and 3x − 2y = 0

The y coefficients are opposite, so add (elimination):

(x + 2y) + (3x − 2y) = 8 + 0  ⇒  4x = 8  ⇒  x = 2

From the first equation: 2 + 2y = 8  ⇒  2y = 6  ⇒  y = 3. They meet at (2, 3).

Example 3 — y = 2x + 1 and 2x − y + 5 = 0

Rewrite the second in slope-intercept form: 2x − y + 5 = 0  ⇒  y = 2x + 5.

Both lines have slope 2 but different intercepts (1 and 5), so they are parallel. They never meet — no intersection.

Example 4 — y = 3x − 2 and 6x − 2y − 4 = 0

Rewrite the second: 6x − 2y − 4 = 0  ⇒  2y = 6x − 4  ⇒  y = 3x − 2.

That is the same equation as the first. The lines are identical, so they share infinitely many points.

Example 5 — y = 2x + 1 and y = −½x + 4

Slopes 2 and −½ multiply to 2 × (−½) = −1, so the lines are perpendicular. Set equal:

2x + 1 = −0.5x + 4  ⇒  2.5x = 3  ⇒  x = 1.2

y = 2(1.2) + 1 = 3.4. They meet at (1.2, 3.4).

7Watch out

Four ways to lose marks

MistakeWhat goes wrongDo this instead
finding x and stoppingThe y-coordinate is left outAlways substitute x back to get y; the answer is a point (x, y)
calling parallel "the same line"Equal slopes alone do not make the lines identicalCheck the intercept too: same slope AND same intercept means identical
subtracting when you should addThe term to be eliminated keeps its signMake coefficients equal and opposite, then add; or equal, then subtract
not checking both equationsAn arithmetic slip passes unnoticedPlug the point into both originals; both must balance

✓ Check yourself before you move on

Four quick questions. Write your answers down first — then open each one.

  1. Find where y = 3x + 2 and y = −x + 6 meet.
    Answer

    3x + 2 = −x + 6 ⇒ 4x = 4 ⇒ x = 1; y = 3(1) + 2 = 5. They meet at (1, 5).

  2. Without drawing, are 4x + 2y = 3 and 2x + y = 5 parallel?
    Answer

    Write both with the same left side: 4x + 2y = 3 and 4x + 2y = 10. Same left side, different right side — equal slopes, different line. Yes, parallel, no intersection.

  3. What does the coefficient test D = a₁b₂ − a₂b₁ tell you when it equals 0?
    Answer

    D = 0 means the slopes are equal. The lines are then either parallel (no solution) or the same line (infinitely many) — you must check the constants to decide which.

  4. Two lines have slopes 3 and −1/3 and meet at (1, 2). Are they perpendicular?
    Answer

    3 × (−1/3) = −1, so yes, they are perpendicular. (The meeting point is extra information, not needed for the perpendicular test.)
