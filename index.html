▶ How to use this site

Read less, think more

This site is built around a simple but slightly uncomfortable idea: you learn more from two minutes of thinking than from ten minutes of reading. So each page deliberately makes you pause and look before it explains anything.

The loop you will follow on every page

  1. Look first. The case study opens with a picture or a situation and no explanation. Spend a minute actually looking at it.
  2. Write what you see in the See box. Only what is there: the shape, the numbers, the direction. No algebra.
  3. Write what you think in the Think box. What rule might be hiding in what you saw? Guess. Being wrong here costs nothing.
  4. Write what you wonder in the Wonder box. Ask the questions you cannot answer yet. Keep them — most get answered by the end of the page.
  5. Then read the mathematics, with your own questions in mind. It will feel different from reading it cold.
  6. Play with the interactive panel. Change one thing at a time and watch what happens to both the graph and the equation.
  7. Do the worked examples yourself first. Write your attempt down before you open the solution. This is the single highest-value habit on this site.
  8. Finish with the self-check and the exit ticket, then rate your confidence and press Next.
Two shortcuts worth knowing

Press → and ← on your keyboard to move between pages. Everything you write is saved automatically as you type — look for the green dot under each box.

★ The thinking routine

See · Think · Wonder

Every case study uses the same three-move routine. It is used in classrooms all over the world because it works: it slows you down, it forces you to separate observation from interpretation, and it makes your own questions the reason you keep reading.

1See

What is actually there?

Describe the picture or the situation as literally as you can. “The line goes down 5 every minute.” “It starts at (0, 40).” Resist the urge to explain. Observations only.

Why it helps: most mistakes in this chapter come from mis-reading the diagram, not from bad algebra.

2Think

What does it make you think?

Now interpret. What relationship might be hiding in what you saw? What would happen if something changed? This is where a guess is genuinely useful — even a wrong one.

Why it helps: a prediction you make yourself is remembered far better than one you are told.

3Wonder

What are you curious about?

Ask questions you cannot answer yet. “Does it matter which point I use?” “What if the slope is zero?” Write them down — they become your reason to read on.

Why it helps: curiosity is what makes the next section feel like an answer rather than a lecture.

There are no wrong answers in the first two boxes

Each box has a Compare with… button that reveals what a strong response might look like. Use it as a mirror, not as a mark scheme — if you noticed something that is not in there, that is a good sign, not a problem. ⚑ Before you begin

What you should already be comfortable with

1Plotting points

Reading and writing coordinates as (x, y) and locating them on a grid.

2Rearranging equations

Moving terms across the equals sign and multiplying through to clear fractions.

3Negative numbers

Comfort with x − (−3) becoming x + 3. This trip up happens constantly.

If any of those feel shaky, page 0 (Prerequisites) revises them from scratch — including the slope formula, which everything else in this course is built on. It is not optional reading.

How long will this take?

About four hours in total if you do the writing. You can do it in one sitting or in twelve short ones — the site remembers where you got to either way. Do not rush the case studies. They look like the slow part and are actually the fast part.

One thing to hold on to

An equation of a line is not a formula to be memorised. It is a test: put in a point, and it tells you whether that point belongs.
