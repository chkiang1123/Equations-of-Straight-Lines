The opening problem (from the chapter)

Jason and Calvin run on the same straight track. They start together; Calvin is 100 m ahead. Jason catches Calvin after 200 s, at a point 700 m from the starting line.

Jason: starts at 0 m, reaches 700 m in 200 s → speed = 700 ÷ 200 = 3.5 m/s.

y = 3.5x

Calvin: starts 100 m ahead, same finish → speed = (700 − 100) ÷ 200 = 3 m/s.

y = 3x + 100

Both travel graphs are straight lines, and the equations are the equations of those lines. That is what this chapter is about: turning a picture of a line into an equation — and back again.

0 Warm-up · what you already know

Four tools we will reuse the whole way

Before building equations, make sure these four are automatic. Everything later is just these, rearranged.

↔Distance

Between A(x1, y1) and B(x2, y2):

AB = √[(x2−x1)² + (y2−y1)²]

Pythagoras, nothing more.

📐Slope

Rise over run, with x1 ≠ x2:

m = y2 − y1x2 − x1

Vertical line → undefined. Horizontal line → 0.

◐Mid-point

Halfway between A and B:

(x1+x22, y1+y22)

Just the average of the coordinates.

∥Parallel / ⟂

Two slopes decide everything:

m1 = m2 → ∥
m3 × m4 = −1 → ⟂

Careful: a vertical line has no slope, so neither rule applies.

Worked mini-example
For P(2, −9) and Q(−4, −1):
Slack of PQ = −1 − (−9)−4 − 2 = 8−6 = −43  ·  PQ = √[(−4−2)² + (−1+9)²] = √(36+64) = √100 = 10
Quick check — try these yourself (answers inside)

(a) Distance between A(2, 9) and B(2, −12) → 21 units (a vertical segment: just 9 − (−12)).

(b) Distance between A(0, −3) and B(−12, 2) → √(144 + 25) = 13.

(c) Slope through A(0, 4), B(3, −1) → −1−43−0 = −53.

(d) P(k, −2), Q(1, −5), PQ = 5: (k−1)² + 9 = 25 → (k−1)² = 16 → k = 5 or −3.

★ The one big idea

A line is a rule that links x and y

Here is the whole chapter in one sentence: a straight line is the set of all points whose coordinates satisfy one equation.

Take the line below, y = 2x + 1. A point is on this line exactly when “its y-coordinate equals 2 × its x-coordinate, plus 1”. That's it.

What you should notice
For points on L, the y-coordinate is always “2 × the x-coordinate, plus 1”. One equation captures infinitely many points.
Two facts worth remembering
(i) Through one point P there are infinitely many straight lines.
(ii) But through P with a given slope there is exactly one straight line. That is why slope + one point is always enough to pin a line down.
1 Form 1 of 5

Point-slope form — the master form

Suppose a line passes through A(x1, y1) and has slope m. Let P(x, y) be any other point on that line. Then the slope of AP must equal m:

  1. Slope of AP = slope of the line = m.
  2. Using the slope formula on A and P: y − y1x − x1 = m.
  3. Multiply both sides by (x − x1) → y − y1 = m(x − x1).
y − y1 = m(x − x1) Point-slope form
y + 1 = −3(x − 3) Drag the sliders →
The blue point is A, the fixed point the line must pass through.

Textbook Example 3.1

A line passes through (3, −1) with slope −3. Find its equation.

Show working

Step 1. Write the point-slope form.

y − y1 = m(x − x1)

Step 2. Substitute m = −3, (x1, y1) = (3, −1).

y − (−1) = −3(x − 3)

Step 3. Expand and tidy up.

y + 1 = −3x + 9 → y = −3x + 8

Answer can also be written 3x + y − 8 = 0.

Common mistake
If the point is (3, −1), then x − x1 becomes x − 3 and y − (−1) becomes y + 1. Keep the minus signs.
2 Form 2 of 5

Two-point form — when you are given two dots

Two points are enough, because two points determine the slope. So we make a slope first, then reuse Step 1.

  1. Find the slope: m = y2 − y1x2 − x1.
  2. Pick either point — A or B, it does not matter — and apply the point-slope form.
y − y1 = y2 − y1x2 − x1(x − x1) Two-point form — same idea as point-slope, slope written out
y = −x + 3 Edit the coordinates →

Point A and Point B — change any number and watch the line re-fit itself.

(−2,5) → (4,−1) (1,10) → (−3,2) (8,−8) → (−6,−1) Origin → (4,8)

Textbook Example 3.2

A(−2, 5) and B(4, −1) — find the equation of the line through both.

Show working

Step 1. Slope:

m = −1 − 54 − (−2) = −66 = −1

Step 2. Take A(−2, 5) and use point-slope:

y − 5 = −1[x − (−2)] → y − 5 = −(x + 2)

Step 3. Simplify:

y − 5 = −x − 2 → y = −x + 3

Using B(4, −1) instead gives the same line — try it.

3 Form 3 of 5

Intercept form — when you are given where the line cuts the axes

If a line cuts the x-axis at (a, 0) and the y-axis at (0, b), then we already have two points — so this is really Step 2 in disguise.

xa + yb = 1 Intercept form · a = x-intercept, b = y-intercept
Where does this come from? (2 lines of algebra)

Take the two points (a, 0) and (0, b). The slope is

b − 00 − a = −ba

Point-slope with (a, 0): y − 0 = −ba(x − a). Divide through by b and rearrange:

xa + yb = 1

y = −2x − 10Drag the intercepts →

Textbook Example 3.3

A line cuts the axes at −5 and −10. Find its equation in slope-intercept form.

Show working

Step 1. Intercept form with a = −5, b = −10:

x−5 + y−10 = 1

Step 2. Isolate y: move the x term over, then multiply by −10.

y−10 = x5 + 1

y = −2x − 10

Watch the signs
Substitute each intercept into the matching position: x-intercept always under x, y-intercept always under y.
4 Form 4 of 5

Slope-intercept form — the one everyone recognises

Take the point-slope form and feed it the y-intercept (0, c):

y − c = m(x − 0)  ⟹  y = mx + c

y = mx + c Slope-intercept form · slope = m, y-intercept = c
y = 1.5x + 2This is the playground — move both sliders
the line y = mx + c y-intercept (0, c) “rise over run” triangle
Positive m → line rises to the right. Negative m → falls. m = 0 → horizontal.

Reading a line straight off the equation

For y = mx + c, the number in front of x is the slope and the standalone number is the y-intercept.

  • y = 2x − 3 → slope 2, y-intercept −3
  • y = −6x + 12 → slope −6, y-intercept 12
  • y = −13x − 21 → slope −⅓, y-intercept −21

Straight lines through the origin

If the y-intercept is 0, the equation collapses to:

y = mx

The origin O(0, 0) is on every such line. Note that this is not the same as y = mx + c with any non-zero c — the line misses the origin by c units.

The handy shortcut
If a horizontal line passes through (a, b), its equation is y = b.
If a vertical line passes through (a, b), its equation is x = a.
You only ever copy one of the two numbers.
6 Form 5 of 5

General form — the shape that fits every line

Every line we have met so far can be written as:

Ax + By + C = 0 General form · A, B, C are constants, A and B not both zero

This is the tidy, standard way to write an answer, and it handles vertical lines too (which y = mx + c cannot).

Reading the features straight out of the general form

Rewrite Ax + By + C = 0 to make y the subject, assuming A ≠ 0 and B ≠ 0:

By = −Ax − C  ⟹  y = −ABx − CB

For the x-intercept put y = 0; for the y-intercept put x = 0.

x-intercept = −CA   ·   y-intercept = −CB   ·   slope = −AB

Two cases to keep in mind

A = 0, B ≠ 0

Then By + C = 0 → y = −C/B — a horizontal line, slope 0.

A ≠ 0, B = 0

Then Ax + C = 0 → x = −C/A — a vertical line, slope undefined.

Slope and inclination

The inclination θ of a line is the angle measured anticlockwise from the positive x-axis to the line, with 0° ≤ θ < 180°. For any non-vertical line:

slope = tan θ   ·   θ = tan−1(slope)
Textbook Example 3.12
For L: 2x − 3y = 0, the slope is ⅔. So tan θ = ⅔ → θ = 33.7° (3 s.f.). A line with the same inclination is parallel to L.

Worked: Example 3.9 (HKDSE 2016 Q25)

L₁: hx + ky + 45 = 0 and L₂: 4x + 6y + 15 = 0 are perpendicular and meet on the x-axis. Find h and k.

Show working

Same x-intercept: −45h = −154 → h = 12

Perpendicular: slope of L₁ × slope of L₂ = −1

(−12k) × (−46) = −1 → k = −8

Check (−1, 5): 12(−1) − 8(5) + 45 = −7 ≠ 0, so (−1, 5) does not lie on L₁.

Worked: perpendicular bisector (Example 3.7)

A(0, 6), B(12, −2). Find the perpendicular bisector of AB.

Show working

Intersections of two straight lines

Given two lines, only three things can happen. The slopes and intercepts tell you which — before you solve anything.

How to actually find the intersection point

Solve the two equations as simultaneous equations. Textbook Example 3.13(c):

Show working: L₁: x + 3y + 7 = 0 and L₂: 2x − y − 21 = 0

x + 3y + 7 = 0  …(1)
2x − y − 21 = 0  …(2)

Eliminate x: compute 2×(1) − (2):

(2x + 6y + 14) − (2x − y − 21) = 0 → 7y + 35 = 0 → y = −5

Substitute back into (1): x + 3(−5) + 7 = 0 → x = 8

Intersection point = (8, −5) ✓

Slopes: −⅓ and 2 are different → exactly one intersection, as expected.

Example 3.15 — "do not intersect"

L₁: 2x − 3y + 12 = 0 and L₂: kx + 6y + 21 = 0 do not intersect. Find k.

Show working

No intersection → parallel → equal slopes:

−2−3 = −k6 → k = −4

The y-intercepts differ (−4 vs −3.5), so the lines really are distinct.

Example 3.14 — intersecting a given line

L₁: x − 3y + 18 = 0 meets the vertical line L₂: x = −9 at P. Find P.

Show working

Substitute x = −9 into L₁:

(−9) − 3y + 18 = 0 → −3y + 9 = 0 → y = 3

So P(−9, 3). And since L₁ cuts the axes at A(−18, 0) and B(0, 6), the mid-point of AB is (−9, 3) — so P is the mid-point of AB.

Reading features out of Ax + By + C = 0

Relationships

  • Parallel: m1 = m2
  • Perpendicular: m1·m2 = −1
  • Perpendicular bisector of AB: through the mid-point of AB, with slope = −1 ÷ (slope of AB)
  • 2 lines: different slopes → 1 point; same slope & different intercept → 0; same slope & same intercept → infinite
The three-step recipe that always works
1. Find the slope (from a point + a point, or a parallel/perpendicular relationship).
2. Find one point on the line (given, mid-point, an intercept, or a bit of geometry).
3. Drop them into the point-slope form and tidy up.
