The point-slope form

One point and one slope. That is genuinely all a straight line needs — and once you see why, the formula stops being something to memorise and becomes something you could have invented yourself.

The draining water tank

A school water tank is being emptied for cleaning. A sensor logs the volume of water left, once a minute. The caretaker plots the readings and gets one perfectly straight line: at the moment the tap is opened there are 40 litres, and the volume falls by exactly 5 litres every minute.

Watch it once all the way through. You can replay any step, or press Autoplay and just watch. Do not do any algebra yet — just look.

1See

Describe only what is actually there — shapes, points, numbers. No explanations, no maths yet.

Compare with what I noticed
  • It is one straight line — not a curve, not broken.
  • It starts at the point (0, 40): zero minutes, 40 litres.
  • It passes through (1, 35), (2, 30), (3, 25), (4, 20) …
  • Every minute it goes down by the same amount: 5 litres.
  • It cuts the horizontal axis at t = 8.
  • Past t = 8 the line would give negative litres — which cannot happen in real life.
2Think

What does it make you think? What rule or relationship might be hiding in what you saw?

Compare with what I thought

A straight line means the change is constant — the same drop every minute. That constant change is the slope.

The point (0, 40) is where the line was at the start. So the whole graph is being carried by just two pieces of information: one point, and one slope.

If that really is all the information there is, then there ought to be a formula that takes (a point, a slope) and hands you back the whole line. That formula is what this page is about.

3The mathematics

Turning an idea into a formula

You have a fixed point and a slope. Let us be completely general about it, so the result works for every line.

Let the fixed point be A(x₁, y₁) and let the slope be m. Now pick any other point on the line and call it P(x, y).

  1. You already know the slope formula. Using A and P as the two points:
    m = y − y₁x − x₁
  2. Multiply both sides by (x − x₁). The denominator disappears:
    y − y₁ = m(x − x₁)
  3. That is the whole derivation. Two lines of algebra — you could have found it.
The point-slope form

A line through (x₁, y₁) with slope m is exactly the set of points satisfying y − y₁ = m(x − x₁).

Read the equation as a sentence

“y − y₁” means how far you have climbed from the known point. “x − x₁” means how far you have walked to the right. The equation simply says: climb = slope × walk. That is all it ever says.

Back to the tank

Slope m = −5 (it is going down, so the slope is negative) and the known point is (0, 40), so x₁ = 0 and y₁ = 40:

V − 40 = −5(t − 0)   ⇒   V = 40 − 5t

And now the equation can answer new questions. Volume after 3.5 minutes? V = 40 − 5(3.5) = 22.5 litres. When is the tank empty? V = 0 gives t = 8 — exactly where the line cut the axis.

5Worked examples

Doing it properly, on paper

Try each one yourself first — write it down. Only then open the solution. Attempting before reading is what makes this stick.

Example 1 — slope 3 through the point (2, 5)

Step 1. Write down what you have: m = 3, and (x₁, y₁) = (2, 5).

Step 2. Put them straight into the form.

y − 5 = 3(x − 2)

Step 3. Expand if the question wants it tidier: y − 5 = 3x − 6, so

y = 3x − 1   or   3x − y − 1 = 0

Both answers are correct. Unless the question says otherwise, the point-slope form on its own is a complete answer.

Example 2 — slope −½ through the point (−3, 4)

Substitute directly: m = −½, x₁ = −3, y₁ = 4.

y − 4 = −12(x + 3)

Notice the trap: x − (−3) became x + 3. Two minus signs in a row make a plus.

Tidying up. Multiply every term by 2 to clear the fraction: 2y − 8 = −(x + 3) = −x − 3. So 2y + x − 5 = 0, or

x + 2y − 5 = 0
Example 3 — the two awkward slopes: 0 and undefined

(a) Slope 0 through (5, −2). Substituting gives y + 2 = 0(x − 5), so y + 2 = 0. The equation of the line is

y = −2

which is a sentence about y only — every point with height −2 is on it, whatever its x. That is a horizontal line.

(b) A vertical line through (5, −2). Here the slope does not exist, because x − x₁ would be zero and you cannot divide by zero. So the point-slope form cannot describe it — and we must write it directly:

x = 5

Remember this exception. It is the single most common reason an exam answer loses a mark.

Example 4 — describing the slope in words (−2, 6), rises 2 for every 3 to the right

The phrase “rises 2 for every 3 to the right” is the slope, written in English: m = 23.

y − 6 = 23(x + 2)

Multiply by 3: 3y − 18 = 2x + 4  ⇒  2x − 3y + 22 = 0.

6The subtle bit

Why your answer may not look like your friend’s

Here is one fixed line, y = 2x − 3. Step through the anchors below: the line must not move — only the equation changes.

The insight

Point-slope form does not give a unique-looking answer — it gives a correct one. Every highlighted point produces a different equation, yet all of them expand to the same y = 2x − 3. So if your answer differs from someone else’s, do not panic: expand both and compare. If they match, you were both right.

✓ Check yourself before you move on

Four quick questions. Write your answers down first — then open each one.

  1. Write the point-slope form of the line with slope 4 through (3, −1).
    Answer
    y + 1 = 4(x − 3)

    The two minuses: y − (−1) = y + 1.

  2. Rewrite y − 7 = −2(x + 3) in slope-intercept form.
    Answer

    Expand: y − 7 = −2x − 6, so

    y = −2x + 1
  3. A line has slope ⅓ and passes through (−6, 2). Does it also pass through (0, 4)?
    Answer

    y − 2 = ⅓(x + 6), so y = ⅓x + 2 + 2 = ⅓x + 4.

    At x = 0, y = 4 — so yes, the line passes through (0, 4). (Notice: this is its y-intercept.)

  4. Are y − 1 = 3(x − 2) and y + 5 = 3(x + 4) the same line?
    Answer

    Expand both. The first gives y = 3x − 5. The second gives y = 3x + 12 − 5 = 3x − 5. Same slope, same intercept: yes, the same line. The two anchors were (2, 1) and (−4, −5) — both sit on it.
